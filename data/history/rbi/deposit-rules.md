# RBI Deposit Interest Rate Rules — Plain-Language Summary

*Compiled 2026-09-27. Companion to `milestones.json` (machine-readable, same facts with exact citations). Every claim below has a source in `milestones.json`; this file just explains them in order. Where sources disagree by a day or two, or where something could not be confirmed, that is flagged explicitly — do not treat this file as more certain than the underlying sources.*

## 1. The regulated era (until 1997 for term deposits, 2011 for savings)

Before the 1990s reforms, RBI told every bank exactly what to pay on every kind of deposit. There was no shopping around for a better FD rate — the rate was the same at every bank.

- **Term deposits.** Deregulation happened gradually, not in one step:
  - 1985: a brief, failed experiment letting banks compete within an 8% ceiling — banks all rushed to 8% even for 15-day deposits, so RBI withdrew the freedom a month later.
  - 1992: RBI replaced maturity-by-maturity rate tables with a single ceiling (13%) for deposits over 46 days, and let banks compete under that ceiling.
  - 1994–1997: the ceiling was adjusted (down to 10%, then up to 12%), and RBI freed longer maturities first — over 2 years in Oct 1995, over 1 year in Jul 1996 — while shorter deposits (30 days–1 year) stayed capped, eventually linked to the Bank Rate.
  - **22 October 1997: full deregulation.** From this date, banks set their own term-deposit rates for every maturity, subject only to their own Board/ALCO sign-off. This is the single most important date for term deposits.
- **Savings deposits** stayed regulated for another 14 years. RBI set one national savings rate for everyone: 4.5% (1978), 5% (1979), 6% (1992 — the all-time high), back down through the 1990s–2000s to 3.5% (2003), up to 4% (May 2011), and then:
  - **25 October 2011: savings deposit rate deregulated** (for resident Indians' domestic accounts). Two rules replaced the single mandated rate and still apply today: (1) the bank must pay the *same* rate to everyone with a balance up to ₹1 lakh; (2) above ₹1 lakh, the bank *may* pay a different (usually higher) rate, but can't discriminate between two similar deposits opened the same day.
  - NRE/NRO savings deposits were deregulated a bit later (documents disagree on the exact date — commercial banks' own circular says NRE on 16 Dec 2011 and NRO on 28 Dec 2011, while a separate urban co-op bank circular says NRE on 25 Nov 2011; not resolved here).
- **1 April 2010** — a smaller but very real-money change: RBI switched the way savings interest is *calculated*, from "minimum balance between the 10th and last day of the month" to "daily product" (i.e., interest on the actual end-of-day balance every single day). This alone raised the real yield on savings accounts even though the *declared* rate didn't move.

## 2. Why your FD rate can legally differ from someone else's

Under the current rules (see §4), a bank is *only* allowed to pay you a different rate than another customer for one of a short, closed list of reasons. If a reason isn't on this list, the bank isn't allowed to use it:

1. **Tenure** — different rates for different lock-in periods (minimum 7 days for domestic deposits; 1 year for NRE; 7 days for NRO).
2. **Size (bulk deposits)** — a bank may pay a different rate only on deposits at or above its "bulk deposit" threshold. That threshold has itself moved over time (see §3) and currently sits at ₹3 crore for most banks. Two carve-outs: this can't be used to pay a *higher* rate on the government's 5-year tax-saving Bank Term Deposit Scheme, 2006, or on Capital Gains Accounts Scheme, 1988 deposits — those must get the ordinary rate.
3. **Non-callable status** — a bank may pay more for a deposit where you give up the right to withdraw early, but only above a threshold (currently ₹1 crore for individuals/HUFs — below that, premature withdrawal must always be allowed).
4. **Staff and certain executives** — banks may add up to 1% extra for their own staff (and for the Chairman/MD/ED etc.) on savings or term deposits.
5. **Senior citizens** — banks may run FD schemes exclusively for resident Indian senior citizens at a higher, fixed rate, on deposits of *any* size (this is the one exception where size doesn't matter). It does not apply if the deposit is legally held by a Hindu Undivided Family (HUF), even if the person managing it (the "Karta") is himself a senior citizen.
6. **Retired staff who are also senior citizens** — get both bonuses stacked (staff benefit + senior-citizen benefit).
7. **Savings-deposit balance tier** — uniform rate up to ₹1 lakh; optional differential rate above ₹1 lakh (this is the Oct 2011 deregulation rule, still in force).
8. **LCR run-off rate (new, from 1 Oct 2026)** — banks may price *bulk* deposits differently depending on how that money is classified under the Liquidity Coverage Ratio framework (see §5).

Everything else — which branch you walked into, whether you negotiated, how big a customer you are outside of the size thresholds above — is explicitly *not* allowed to change your rate. Rates must match a schedule the bank has published in advance; they are not negotiable.

## 3. How the "bulk deposit" and "non-callable" thresholds moved over time

These are two *related but distinct* numbers, and press coverage sometimes conflates them:

- **"Bulk deposit" (the size threshold that lets a bank quote you a different rate at all):** ₹15 lakh (from 1998) → ₹1 crore (from 1 April 2013, formally defined as "Bulk Deposit" in the March 2016 Master Direction) → ₹2 crore (22 Feb 2019) → **₹3 crore (7 June 2024, current)**. Regional Rural Banks and Local Area Banks have their own, lower threshold: ₹15 lakh → ₹1 crore (26 Oct 2023).
- **"Non-callable minimum" (the size above which a bank is allowed to sell you a deposit with NO early-withdrawal right at all):** individuals/HUFs were always protected below some threshold; that threshold was ₹1 crore blanket (2013) → ₹15 lakh (16 April 2015) → **₹1 crore (26 Oct 2023, current)**. In other words, today, any FD of ₹1 crore or less that you hold as an individual must let you withdraw early (subject to the bank's penalty policy) — a bank cannot force a no-withdrawal deposit on you below that amount.

## 4. The current rulebook (as of 27 September 2026)

The rules above are currently written down in the **Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks – Interest Rate on Deposits) Directions, 2025** (dated 28 November 2025), and a parallel, nearly identical **Small Finance Banks** version issued the same day. (A short-lived single combined Direction for all bank types existed from 1 April to 28 November 2025, then RBI split it back into one Direction per bank category — commercial banks, SFBs, payments banks, local area banks, RRBs, urban co-op banks, rural co-op banks all now have their own document.)

Structure of the Commercial Banks Directions: general framework → domestic current/savings/term deposits (with the reasons-for-variation list in §2) → non-resident (NRE/NRO) rupee deposits → foreign-currency (FCNR(B)) deposits → prohibitions/exemptions.

## 5. The 2026 amendments (three so far)

1. **17 June 2026 — "Amendment Directions, 2026."** A temporary relaxation to attract long-term foreign-currency inflows: for a limited window, fresh NRE deposits of 3 years or more don't have to stay below the domestic rate, and the FCNR(B) rate ceiling for 3–5 year deposits was lifted.
2. **30 July 2026 — "Second Amendment Directions, 2026" (effective 1 October 2026).** This is the headline 2026 change for ordinary savers, in two parts:
   - Every rate a bank pays — on *any* deposit, including bulk deposits — must strictly match the schedule it has already published on its website. No more quietly paying a different rate than advertised.
   - **Bulk-deposit rates specifically must be posted on the bank's website by 10:00 am, with a 10-minute grace period (so, absolutely no later than 10:10 am), every single business day.** This was introduced after scrutiny of bulk-deposit pricing practices (RBI's own published feedback document references the HDFC Bank/MSRDC "marketing spend" episode as context). In exchange, banks were given new freedom to price *bulk* deposits differently based on the deposit's Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) "run-off rate" classification — i.e., how flighty/sticky that kind of money is considered for the bank's liquidity buffer.
3. **25 August 2026 — "Third Amendment Directions, 2026."** A technical tightening only: it moved the end-date of the 17-June temporary NRE/FCNR(B) relaxation earlier, from 30 September 2026 to 31 August 2026. No new substantive rule.

## 6. Gaps — what this research could NOT confirm

- **Before 1970:** no RBI table with actual deposit-rate *levels* was found. The task brief's hint that RBI started prescribing bank deposit rates "around 1964" is not independently confirmed here — RBI's own 1964-dated action found in this pass concerned regulation of *non-banking* companies' deposit-taking, not commercial banks. RBI's *History, Volume II (1951–1967)*, chapter "Regulating Banks and Deposit Institutions," is the most likely place to resolve this and was not opened in this pass.
- **Savings-deposit rates before 1977** are not sourced from an RBI primary document in this pass (see `savings_rate_history.json` gap notes) — the 1977–2003 figures come from a secondary technical report that itself cites RBI policy statements; verify against the RBI Annual Report / Credit Policy statement text before publishing on the tracker.
- **A median/modal card-rate series and a savings-deposit-rate-range series**, which the task brief hypothesized might exist in the monthly "Lending and Deposit Rates" release, were **not found** in that release's actual data workbook (only weighted-average WADTDR/WALR and MCLR appear there). If RBI publishes those elsewhere, it wasn't identified in this pass.
- **DBIE (data.rbi.org.in)** is a JavaScript application; a plain script cannot pull data from it without a browser or an undocumented API — confirmed directly in this pass, not merely assumed.
